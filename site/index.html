FloodNet NYC · sensor data since 2020

When New York's streets flood, these sensors are watching.

FloodNet is a network of small sensors on street signs across all five boroughs. Each one checks the water depth below it every minute. Use this page to see where streets flood, how deep the water gets, how often it happens, and what the City is doing about it.

How deep is "flooded"?

A flood event starts when water collects under a sensor and ends when it drains away. Most events are shallow. But one inch of street water at the wrong moment can still pour into a basement apartment.

    Two kinds of flooding

    Not every flood comes from rain. Along Jamaica Bay, City Island, and Staten Island's shore, high tides push water up through storm drains and over bulkheads, sometimes on sunny days. These floods follow the moon's schedule. Rain floods follow the weather. Each kind needs a different fix.

    How fast does the water come and go?

    Every flood has a shape: water rises to a peak, then drains. If it rises fast, the sewers were overwhelmed in minutes. If it drains slowly, the water had nowhere to go. Tap any dot to see the full shape of that flood.

    Neighborhoods

    Floods per sensor-year adjusts for how many sensors an area has and how long they've been up. That keeps a neighborhood with ten sensors from looking ten times worse than one with a single sensor.

    Sensors vs. 311 calls

    Before FloodNet, the City learned about street flooding mostly from 311 calls, often after the damage was done. Sensors report on their own, every minute, including at night and in places where people rarely call.

    What the City is doing

    NYC is working on flooding in several ways: bigger sewers, "cloudburst" sponge projects, green infrastructure, coastal walls and raised shorelines, and better warnings. Filter by borough, or tap a sensor on the map to see projects that apply there.

    How FloodNet helps NYC

    Warnings in real time

    NYC Emergency Management and the National Weather Service see sensor readings as floods happen, which helps them target warnings and send crews.

    Proof that fixes work

    A sensor that floods before a new sewer or cloudburst project, and stops flooding after, is direct evidence the money worked. Sensors are already in place near many planned projects.

    Seeing tidal flooding

    Sunny-day tidal floods rarely make the news. The sensors show they happen dozens of times a year in parts of Queens, the Bronx, and Staten Island.

    Community-chosen locations

    Residents can suggest a sensor location. The data is free to everyone.

    Get the data

    Open data used here

    • FloodNet: Street Flooding Events. One row per flood, with the full depth curve (NYC Open Data, updated every 2 weeks).
    • FloodNet: Sensor Deployment Metadata. Location, install date, and whether the site is tidal.
    • 311 Service Requests. Sewer complaints for street flooding, clogged catch basins, and highway flooding.
    • Open-Meteo historical weather. Modeled hourly rain for Central Park.
    • floodnet.nyc and the live FloodNet dashboard.

    Things to know

    • A flood event is a run of readings deeper than 10 mm (about 0.4 in.). The FloodNet team checks every event before publishing it.
    • The sensors measure water directly below them, at one point on one street. Nearby blocks can be deeper or drier.
    • Rain-driven vs. tidal on this page uses the "tidally influenced" flag on each sensor. A tidal site can still flood from rain.
    • Times are shown in New York time. The published data uses GMT.
